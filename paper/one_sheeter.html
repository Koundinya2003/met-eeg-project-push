The Song or the Self?

A preregistered search for personal musical emotion in scalp EEG

Aditya Koundinya · Independent researcher, Bengaluru · October 2026
0 of 3 supported

The short version. Twenty people heard the same twenty songs. When a song moved one listener more than it moved them usually, or more than it moved everyone else, their alpha rhythm showed no detectable change. The song is easy to find in the ratings. The self is much harder to find in the brain.

The setup

Same songs, different feelings

Music can make a whole room feel different things at once. If the brain carries a signature of emotion, it should follow what each listener actually felt, not only what the song was built to make them feel.

20listeners, aged 21 to 29
20songs, 2 minutes each
66EEG channels
395trials after quality control

Public MET Music Induced EEG Dataset (OpenNeuro). After each song, listeners rated valence and arousal on a 1 to 9 scale.

Split every rating into song and self

Each rating was broken into the part everyone shared and the part only that listener had.

Three bets, written down before looking
H1NOT SUPPORTED

More aroused than usual → lower posterior alpha

Alpha, the 8 to 13 Hz rhythm at the back of the head, drops when the cortex gets busier. It did not drop. If anything it rose very slightly, the wrong way.

slope 0.05 · p = .355
H2NOT SUPPORTED

More pleasant than usual → more left frontal activity

Frontal alpha asymmetry is the classic “approach and positive mood” marker. Its link to how pleasant a song felt sat almost exactly on zero.

slope 0.01 · p = .903
H3NOT SUPPORTED

People differ in how strong these links are

Maybe the effect runs one way for some and the other way for others? The spread between people came out at essentially zero. What looked like individual differences was noise from only 20 songs each.

between person SD ≈ 0.000 (H1), 0.006 (H2)

Standardized within person effects with 95% intervals. The shaded side of each row is where the hypothesis predicted the dot would land. Every interval straddles zero, including the person specific tests that used only the personal deviation.

There is plenty of personal feeling in the ratings, especially for valence. But any emotional effect in the brain had to show up in alpha’s thin 6% slice, alongside alertness, fatigue and attention.

Why this null still says something

The intervals are narrow. A one standard deviation rise in how aroused you feel is unlikely to lower your posterior alpha by more than about 0.05 standard deviations.

For valence, effects larger than about 0.11 SD in either direction are unlikely. If these links exist at the level of whole songs, they are small.

A lesson in counting

395 trials are not 395 people

Pool every trial as if independentr = 0.009, falsely tight
One slope per person, then combiner = 0.08, honest width
Correlate 20 personal averagesa different question

Treating trials as people (pseudoreplication) claims precision the data do not have. Mixed models with listeners as the unit fixed that.

Pushing on the result

It held every way it was poked

Both ratings in one modelnull
Adding liking and familiaritynull
Relative instead of absolute powernull
Stricter 75 µV artifact thresholdnull
Leaving out each listener in turn (×20)null

H1 slope stayed between 0.03 and 0.06, every p above .25.

Why the textbook effects may have vanished
The comparison changed. Older studies compared happy songs with sad ones, so tempo, loudness and timbre came along for the ride. Holding the song fixed strips that away.
The timescale is coarse. One EEG value per two minutes and one rating after the music ends. A chill lasting a few seconds is diluted almost to nothing.
The signal is small next to the noise. Alpha differs hugely between people, and no song was truly unpleasant (none averaged below 3.1 of 9), so valence had little room to act.
What this does not show

It does not show that EEG is blind to musical emotion. It shows that two widely cited alpha markers, averaged over whole songs in twenty people, did not follow personal feeling. That is a narrower and more useful conclusion.

Built in honesty: hypotheses, outcomes, exclusion rules and thresholds were committed to version control on 6 October 2026, before any EEG to rating analysis ran. Three deviations are reported openly. The data also had surprises (undocumented notch filter, a truncated and a missing recording, unrecoverable play order), all logged rather than hidden.
